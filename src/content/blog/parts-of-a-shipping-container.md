faq:
  - q: "What are corner castings used for?"
    a: "Corner castings are the eight steel blocks at a container's corners. Crane spreaders, twistlocks and lashing gear connect to them. They follow ISO 1161, so standard gear fits any standard box."
  - q: "Why are shipping container walls corrugated?"
    a: "Corrugated means pressed into ridges. The ridges make thin wall steel much stiffer than a flat sheet of the same thickness."
  - q: "Is it safe to cut a hole in a container wall?"
    a: "A container's steel frame carries the main loads, and the walls are part of what the box is strength tested on. Any cut changes the structure, so talk to the manufacturer or a licensed engineer first."
  - q: "Do 40ft containers have forklift pockets?"
    a: "No. The 1990 ISO container standard says forklift pockets shall not be fitted on 40ft containers, and current guides agree. Some 20ft containers have them. They can lift the box empty, and loaded only where that is specifically permitted."
  - q: "What is a gooseneck tunnel on a container?"
    a: "A gooseneck tunnel is a recessed channel under the floor at one end of some 40ft containers. It fits over the raised front of a gooseneck trailer, so the box rides about 150 mm lower on the truck."
  - q: "What does the CSC plate on a container show?"
    a: "The CSC plate is a permanent safety approval plate. It lists the approval reference and build date, the manufacturer's ID number, maximum gross weight, allowable stacking weight, racking test force, and the next exam date or an ACEP mark."

**The main parts of a shipping container are a steel frame with eight corner castings, corrugated steel walls and roof, a hardwood plywood floor on steel crossmembers, two cargo doors with locking bars, forklift pockets or a gooseneck tunnel on some boxes, small air vents, and its markings, including the CSC safety plate.** The frame carries the load. The other parts close the box in. The explorer below breaks these groups into 11 parts you can tap or click.

Thickness, weight and other spec figures below come from published builder specs and standards. Your box may differ.

## The frame

**The frame is the steel skeleton of a shipping container, and it is the part that carries the load.**

Picture a box drawn with thick steel lines. Those lines are the frame:

- **4 corner posts**, one at each upright edge
- **2 top side rails** and **2 bottom side rails** along the long sides
- **2 bottom end rails** across the ends (the one under the doors is the **door sill**)
- **A front top end rail**, plus a **door header** across the top of the doors

Frame steel is much thicker than wall steel. The corner posts are about 6 mm thick and the bottom side rails are 4.5 mm.

**What to look at on a used box:** Walk along the bottom side rails, where the walls meet the base. Heavy rust or holes there deserve a close look.

### Corner castings

**Corner castings are the eight steel blocks at the corners of a container, where lifting and securing gear connects.**

They're also called corner fittings. They're cast steel, made to a standard called ISO 1161, so standard gear fits any standard box. Crane spreaders lock into the four top castings. Twistlocks (turning locks that clip one box to the one below), lashing rods and stacking cones connect to them too.

Corner castings let a container move between ships, trains and trucks without anyone unloading the cargo.

**What to look at:** Check all eight castings. They should be whole, with no cracks, broken edges or bent spots.

## Walls and roof

**The walls and roof are thin sheets of corrugated steel, joined to the frame, that keep the weather out.**

"Corrugated" means pressed into ridges, like the folds in a paper fan. The ridges make a thin sheet much stiffer than a flat sheet of the same thickness.

Standard dry containers typically have no insulation in the walls. That's one reason a closed box can [sweat or "rain" inside](/blog/why-does-my-container-rain-inside-condensation/).

**What to look at:** Look over the wall and roof panels for holes or rust-through.

### How thick is shipping container steel?

Thin. The side wall panels are about 1.6 to 2.0 mm thick.

One spec lists SPA-H steel, a weathering steel like the kind sold under the trade name Cor-Ten. It's made to resist rust better than plain steel.

The main ISO standard for cargo containers, ISO 1496-1 (1990 edition), tests the walls by pushing on them. Each side wall gets 0.6 times the box's payload, and each end wall gets 0.4 times.

The same standard says an opening must not weaken a wall's ability to pass those tests. So any cut changes the structure. Thinking about adding a door or window? Talk to the manufacturer or a licensed engineer first.

### Can you stand on the roof?

Yes, carefully. The roof is corrugated steel sheets welded into one panel, with a slight upward camber. The ISO standard tests it for people working on it: 300 kg spread over a small patch, 600 by 300 mm.

But the roof is not a storage deck. **Never put cargo on it.**

## Floor and crossmembers

**The floor is thick hardwood plywood screwed down onto steel beams called crossmembers.**

Crossmembers are C-shaped steel beams that run side to side under the floor, like the joists under a house floor. One 20ft spec lists 18 of them.

### What is the floor made of?

Most container floors are hardwood plywood, about 25 to 30 mm thick (about 1 to 1.2 inches). Specs list 25, 28 or 30 mm, or 1 1/8 inch marine plywood. One spec calls for at least 19 layers of hardwood, such as keruing or apitong.

Builder specs test the floor with a test truck loaded to 7,260 kg (16,000 lb). For what your own box can safely hold, ask its manufacturer or a licensed engineer. We do not issue load ratings.

**What to look at:** Walk the floor and press down in a few spots. It should feel solid, not soft or springy.

## Doors and locking gear

**The cargo doors are two steel panels at one end, each held shut by two locking bars that turn into cam keepers.**

On the spec we used, the doors have:

- **Two door leaves**, each on 4 hinges. Each swings back about 270 degrees. The right door opens first.
- **Four locking bars**, also called locking rods, two per door.
- **Cams and keepers.** Each bar ends in a cam, a shaped catch. Turn the handle and the cams twist into keepers in the door header (top) and door sill (bottom).
- **Gaskets.** Double-lip rubber seals (EPDM, a weatherproof rubber) that squeeze tight when the doors are latched.
- **A customs seal point** on the locking gear, which is hot-dip galvanized (zinc-coated against rust).

The bars and cams hold the doors closed. A separate lock adds security, and a seal shows whether the doors have been opened.

**What to look at:** Torn, cracked or missing gasket rubber can let water reach your stored goods. The doors should line up and close evenly, and each locking bar should turn and seat in its keepers without a fight. Tight gaskets and working locking gear are the heart of a [Wind & Water Tight](/blog/wind-and-water-tight-explained/) box.

## Forklift pockets and gooseneck tunnel

**Forklift pockets are two slots through the base of some 20ft containers, sized for a forklift's forks.**

The same ISO standard allows pockets on a 20ft box for lifting the box loaded or empty. Some boxes add a second, inner set for empty boxes only. Lift a loaded box by its pockets only where that is specifically permitted. The standard also says forklift pockets shall not be fitted on 40ft containers.

**A gooseneck tunnel is a recessed channel under the floor at one end of some 40ft containers.** It fits over the raised front of a gooseneck trailer, so the box rides about 150 mm lower on the truck.

**What to look at:** If you plan to move your box with a forklift, check for pockets first.

## Vents

**Vents are small covered openings in the side walls that let a little air move in and out.**

Both builder specs we checked list two. In one, they're small ABS plastic vents, one in each side wall near a corner post. They even out air pressure and help a little with [condensation](/blog/why-does-my-container-rain-inside-condensation/). On their own, vents usually can't clear damp air out of a closed box.

**What to look at:** Vents should be in place, not blocked or broken.

## Markings and the CSC plate

**Markings are the codes painted on a container, plus a safety plate, that identify the box and its limits.**

The big ID code follows a standard called ISO 6346. Our guide on [how to read a container's ID number](/blog/how-to-read-container-id-number-iso-6346/) decodes it, and our [check digit calculator](/iso-6346-check-digit-calculator/) tests any ID for typos.

**The CSC plate is a permanent safety approval plate that shows a container's approved limits.** Under the international CSC rules, as written into UK law, the plate must be at least 200 by 100 mm, permanent, non-corroding and fireproof. It lists:

- the approval reference and build date
- the manufacturer's ID number
- maximum gross weight
- allowable stacking weight at 1.8 g
- racking test force (how hard the box was pushed sideways)
- the next exam date, or an ACEP mark, meaning the box is on an approved ongoing inspection plan

On many 20ft plates, the max gross weight is 30,480 kg. Since a 2016 update, ISO 668 allows up to 36,000 kg. The CSC plate gives the actual figure for each box.

**What to look at:** If the CSC plate's exam date has lapsed and you need it current for shipping, see [how to get a used container certified](/blog/get-a-used-shipping-container-certified/).

## 20ft vs 40ft vs 40ft high cube: what changes

The parts are the same on all three: frame, corner castings, walls, roof, floor, doors and vents. These details change:

| Part or measure | 20ft | 40ft | 40ft High Cube |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outside length | 6,058 mm (about 20 ft) | 12,192 mm (40 ft) | 12,192 mm (40 ft) |
| Outside height | 2,591 mm (8'6") | 2,591 mm (8'6") | 2,896 mm (9'6") |
| Forklift pockets | Optional, on some boxes | Not fitted | Not fitted |
| Gooseneck tunnel | No | Optional | Optional |

That extra foot (about 305 mm) gives a typical High Cube about 2,697 to 2,700 mm of height inside.

Our [20ft vs 40ft comparison](/blog/20ft-vs-40ft-shipping-container/) helps you pick a size. For full measurements, see our [shipping container dimensions chart](/blog/shipping-container-dimensions-size-chart/) or the [Container Reference](/container-reference/).
