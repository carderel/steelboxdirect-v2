A steel container can give a Charlotte home, shop or jobsite a lot of dry, locked space. The box itself is the easy part. The real work is in three places: your rules, your ground, and the road in.

This post is a checklist for all three. It goes one step at a time. If you want to see sizes, stock and our delivery area first, start with our page on [shipping containers for sale in Charlotte](/locations/north-carolina/charlotte-shipping-containers/). Then come back here to plan.

## Step 1: Know what you are buying

We stock one grade: **used Wind and Water Tight (WWT)**. That means the box keeps out rain, wind and pests, and the doors seal. It is not new. Expect some rust, dents and scuffs from its years of cargo service. Our [condition guide](/condition/) shows what normal wear looks like.

A few things are special request only:

- **New (one-trip) containers**
- **Cargo Worthy containers**
- **10ft containers**

We sell plain storage containers. We do not sell or install heaters, and we do not modify boxes.

Our standard sizes are 20ft and 40ft. A 40ft has about twice the floor space, but it needs a much bigger spot and a longer path for the truck. Our [20ft vs 40ft guide](/blog/20ft-vs-40ft-shipping-container/) helps you choose.

## Step 2: Read your HOA papers first

Lots of homes around Charlotte sit in neighborhoods with a homeowners association. If yours does, start here. An HOA "no" can stop a project even when the city or town has no problem with it.

In North Carolina, many of these neighborhoods are "planned communities," and a state law, Chapter 47F of the General Statutes, applies to many of them. Condos fall under a different law, Chapter 47C. Each one has a **declaration**. That is a set of lot rules that was recorded with the county.

Where to find it:

- Your closing papers from when you bought the house
- Your HOA's website or management company
- The Mecklenburg County Register of Deeds, which keeps recorded documents

What to look for:

- Any rules about sheds, outbuildings or storage units
- Any need for approval from an architecture or design committee
- Rules on screening, fences or where things can sit on the lot

Then ask the board or the management company in writing. Keep their answer. **We do not read or interpret HOA rules for you.** Your HOA is the one who decides.

## Step 3: Find out who handles zoning for your address

In Mecklenburg County, zoning and building permits are two different jobs, and they can sit with two different offices. Where you live decides who you call.

**Inside Charlotte city limits.** Zoning questions go to the City of Charlotte. The City of Charlotte's residential permit packet has a zoning review step, so ask Planning, Design and Development whether it applies to your plans. That is a good sign that zoning and building are separate steps here. Our [North Carolina permits page lists the City of Charlotte office](/permits/north-carolina/#city-of-charlotte) with a link.

**In Davidson, Cornelius, Huntersville, Matthews, Mint Hill or Pineville.** Each of these towns does its own zoning. If you live in one of them, your town is where zoning questions start.

**Building permits.** Mecklenburg County Code Enforcement handles building permits for Charlotte and for the towns of Davidson, Cornelius, Huntersville, Matthews, Mint Hill and Pineville. Its phone line is 980-314-CODE (2633). See the [Mecklenburg County entry](/permits/north-carolina/#mecklenburg-county) on our permits page.

**One town to watch: Stallings.** Stallings sits in both Mecklenburg and Union counties. The Town of Stallings handles its own zoning, and the town's permit page says Union County Building Code Enforcement issues building permits. If you live in Stallings, ask the town which offices handle your address.

Not sure which one you are in? Give the county office your street address and ask.

When you call, go in with good questions. Our permits page has a short list to ask from, like how they treat plain storage, how long the box can stay, and how setbacks are measured on your lot.

**We do not decide whether you need a permit.** Your city or town and your county do. Checking with them before you buy is the buyer's job.

## Step 4: Look up your lot on the flood map

Charlotte-Mecklenburg Storm Water Services runs an online tool called the **3D Interactive Floodzone Map**. Type in your address and it shows your lot.

It shows two kinds of floodplain:

- **FEMA floodplain:** where flooding is likely now
- **Community floodplain:** where flooding is likely in the future, as more land upstream gets built on

That second one matters in a growing area. Flood risk there is expected to grow as land upstream is built on, so check both layers, not only the FEMA one.

Have a question about what you see? Call Storm Water Services at **704-432-RAIN (7246)**. Charlotte's development rules have their own article on floodplains, and Storm Water Services takes floodplain questions, so if your spot is in one, ask them how it affects your plans. That call is yours to make, not ours.

## Step 5: Get the red clay ground ready

Our [Charlotte page](/locations/north-carolina/charlotte-shipping-containers/) covers the basics of Piedmont clay. Here is a closer look at why it matters for a steel box.

The USDA keeps detailed notes on local soil types. One of them is called the **Mecklenburg series**. The USDA lists its clay subsoil as "firm, sticky, plastic." Water moves through it slowly. It also has a moderate "shrink-swell" rating. That means it swells when wet and shrinks when dry, so the ground can shift a little over the seasons.

New-build lots add one more problem. Heavy equipment packs the dirt down while houses go up. NC State Extension notes that this kind of packing makes clay drain even worse. So a fresh lot in a new subdivision can stay soggy longer than you might think.

What that means for your spot:

- **Go high.** Pick a spot above the low parts of the yard. Stay out of the path where rain sheets across the lawn.
- **Go level.** A container sits on its four corners. If one corner is low, the doors can bind. Level side to side matters most. Our [delivery page](/delivery/) explains why.
- **Lift it off the clay.** A gravel pad or solid blocks under the corners help keep the steel off wet ground and let the underside dry.
- **Ask for blocking up front.** If you want the box set on blocks, say so when you ask for a quote. It changes how the box comes off the truck.
- **Get help for big jobs.** A local grading or landscape contractor can look at slope and drainage on your lot.
- **Call 811 before you dig.** Call 811 or 1-800-632-4949 at least three working days before you dig for a pad. NC811 has public utility lines marked for free. Private lines on your property are not marked.

Fall is a fair time to do this work. On average, October is one of Charlotte's two driest months, along with February. The gap is small, though. Charlotte still sees about 73 days a year with a tenth of an inch of rain or more. So plan for wet days anyway.

One more thing about a steel box in a humid place: water drops can form on the inside walls. That is usually condensation, not a leak. Here is [why a container "rains" inside and how to stop it](/blog/why-does-my-container-rain-inside-condensation/).

## Step 6: Walk the path the truck will take

A container delivery rig is big. It is roughly a sixty-five foot truck combination. It needs a long, straight way in, enough width, room overhead, and ground firm enough to hold it. Our [delivery page](/delivery/) lists the exact numbers.

Take a walk from the street to your spot. Look for:

- **Low branches and wires.** Street trees and overhead lines are common in older Charlotte neighborhoods.
- **Tight turns.** A cul-de-sac or a sharp bend in the driveway can be hard for a long truck.
- **Fences and gates.** Measure the gate. Check if a fence section can come out.
- **Soft spots.** A lawn over wet clay may not hold a loaded truck, even if it looks dry on top.
- **Things in the way.** Mailboxes, parked cars, play sets and landscape beds.
- **Door room.** Leave space in front of the doors so they can swing all the way open.

Most deliveries use a tilt-bed truck. It slides the box off the back as it pulls forward. When a site is too tight or too soft for that, a crane-set delivery can lift the box into place instead.

You do not have to figure this out alone. We plan placement with you and can talk through tight or soft sites before we schedule. Tell us about any trouble spots before you commit, not on delivery day.
