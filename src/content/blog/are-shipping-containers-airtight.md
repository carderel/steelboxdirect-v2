**No. A shipping container is not airtight.** A Wind and Water Tight (WWT) container keeps out rain, wind, snow, and pests. That is a different job from sealing out air.

But here's the part that matters: "not airtight" does not mean "well ventilated." A closed steel box can still trap fumes and run short on fresh air.

That one gap explains most of what follows. It's why you never heat a closed container with fuel. It's why you air out a box before you walk in. And it's why winter storage takes a little planning.

## Wind and water tight is not airtight

WWT is the grade we stock. It means the box has no holes and its doors seal, so it keeps the weather out. Read [what "Wind & Water Tight" actually means](/blog/wind-and-water-tight-explained/) for the full breakdown, or see our [condition guide](/condition/).

<!-- hs003-allow: describes the container condition grade (WWT), not a structure classification or a structural or load rating -->
WWT is a weather-and-water grade, not an airtightness rating. The cargo doors close against rubber gaskets. Those gaskets keep out rain and wind, but they are not an airtight seal. Worn gaskets can leak, and humid outside air can still find its way in. Some containers also have small vents up high near the roof. Others have none.

So a little air moves in and out. Think of a closed car parked in a garage. It isn't airtight either. But you'd never run the engine with the windows up and expect the small gaps to save you.

## Why that matters: "a little air" is not ventilation

The slow leak through door seals is not enough to clear anything out. If something inside makes fumes, those fumes build up faster than they leak away.

This isn't a guess. The UK Health and Safety Executive (HSE) measured the air inside freight containers at ports and distribution centres. It found toxic substances and low oxygen levels in some of them. HSE says the build-up depends on the contents, their condition and the length of time in transit.

The lesson for an owner is simple. Treat a closed container like a space that may hold bad air. Open it up and let it breathe before you step inside, especially if it's been shut for a while.

## Heaters, generators, and engines: never inside a closed box

This is the big one. Do not run anything that burns fuel inside a closed container. That means:

- Generators
- Propane, kerosene, or gas space heaters
- Lawn mowers, snowblowers, ATVs, or vehicles left running
- Grills, camp stoves, or charcoal of any kind

Burning fuel makes carbon monoxide (CO). The CDC calls CO "an odorless, colorless gas that kills without warning." You can't see it or smell it. Early signs feel like the flu: headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, and confusion.

The CDC is blunt about generators: never use one inside a home or garage, "even if doors and windows are open." Keep it outside, more than 20 feet from doors, windows, and vents. A container with its doors cracked is no safer than a garage.

The US EPA lists unvented kerosene and gas space heaters, generators, and other gas-powered equipment as common CO sources. Burning fuel also uses up oxygen. In a small, closed space, both problems stack up.

### What about warming an engine before you drive it out?

Don't. Pull it out or open both doors wide before you start it. A running engine inside a steel box is exactly the kind of CO source the CDC and EPA warn about. The CDC lists car and truck engines and small engines among the main sources of CO.

### So how do you heat a container?

That's a job for a qualified installer, not a blog post.

Heat, wiring, and venting all have to be planned together for your setup and your local codes. We don't give heater or electrical how-to, and we don't sell or install heaters. If you want a heated space, talk to a licensed HVAC pro or electrician first.

## Imported boxes and fumigation: air it out first

Many used containers spent years hauling cargo overseas. Some of that cargo gets treated with fumigant gas to kill bugs during shipping. The fumigant is poison by design.

Rules say a fumigated container should carry a warning label. But the HSE warns that "absence of marking cannot be taken to mean fumigants are not present." It also notes that boxes marked as aired out can still hold gas that soaked into the cargo and seeped back out.

The EU's workplace safety agency (EU-OSHA) adds that some fumes come from cargo itself, not from fumigation. Solvents like toluene and benzene have been found in shipped containers.

### What this means for you

A container sold for storage is empty and has been opened and handled many times. The risk is mainly about boxes still sealed with cargo inside. Still, good habits cost nothing:

- When a box arrives, open both doors and let it air out before you walk in.
- If you smell anything chemical or see a fumigation warning sign, stay out and step back. Some fumigants have no smell, so no smell does not prove the air is safe.
- Don't send a kid or a pet in first to "check it out."
- If you feel dizzy or get a headache inside, get out into fresh air right away.

If you ever buy a container still loaded with cargo, that's a different situation. HSE guidance says a suspect box should be opened with a trained, competent person present and tested before anyone goes in.

## Fuels and solvents: fumes build up in storage too

You don't need a running engine to get bad air. Gas cans, paint thinner, solvents, and propane tanks can all leak vapor. In a closed box, those vapors have nowhere to go.

That's why propane, batteries, paint, and solvents sit on our list of [12 things you should never store in a shipping container](/blog/12-things-never-store-in-a-shipping-container/).

If you must keep fuel-powered gear like a mower inside, check its manual for safe storage and keep spare fuel elsewhere. Your local fire department can tell you what's allowed where you live.

## Summer heat: the box gets hot

A closed steel container in full sun heats up like a car in a parking lot. In full sun, the air inside a closed container can get hotter than the air outside. Heat also makes some stored items give off more fumes.

Two easy habits help. Open the doors and let hot air out before you go in. And don't stay inside a closed, hot container for long. Treat it like a parked car in July.

## Winter storage: what works and what to watch

Winter is a good time to use a container. Patio furniture, holiday decorations, garden tools, and [building supplies](/for/contractors/) all store well. A few things to plan for:

**Condensation.** When warm, damp air meets cold steel, it turns into water drops on the ceiling. It's not a leak. We cover the why and the fixes in [why a container "rains" inside](/blog/why-does-my-container-rain-inside-condensation/), so we won't repeat it here.

**Ventilation basics.** A little airflow helps with both moisture and stale air. Passive vents placed high and low are the usual first step. Adding vents means cutting steel, so have a qualified installer do it.

**Keep it off the floor.** Use sealed plastic totes and shelves. Keep paper, cardboard, and fabric off the cold floor and away from the walls.

**What to leave out in winter:**

- Anything that freezes and breaks, like canned goods, water-based paint, or liquid cleaners
- Fuel cans and propane tanks
- Food of any kind
- Electronics you can't afford to lose to moisture

**Never warm it up with a fuel heater.** It's tempting on a cold day when you're working in there. Don't. Same rule as above.

## The short version

- **Airtight?** No. WWT keeps weather out, not air.
- **Safe air?** Not on its own. A closed box can trap fumes and run low on oxygen.
- **Heaters and engines?** Never fuel-burning ones inside a closed container.
- **Imported box?** Open both doors and air it out before you go in.
- **Winter?** Plan for condensation, keep fuels out, and keep stuff off the floor.
