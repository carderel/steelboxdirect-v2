Fall is when a lot of us look at the yard and think, "Where is all this stuff going to go for winter?" The mower. The patio chairs. The kids' bikes. The tools that have lived in the garage since spring.

Two answers come up again and again: build a shed, or get a shipping container. Both can work. This guide is a fair look at each one, plus one thing that is easy to overlook: the calendar. In Ohio, Indiana, Kentucky and West Virginia, late fall is a harder time to build.

## The honest answer to "which is cheaper?"

It depends. That is not a dodge. It is the real answer, because the sticker is only one part of the cost.

Here is what goes into each one.

**A shed:**

- The shed kit, or the lumber and roofing to build one
- A base: gravel, blocks, or a concrete slab
- Labor, or your own weekends
- Tools you may need to buy or rent
- Delivery, if you buy a ready-built shed

**A used shipping container:**

- The container
- Delivery to your ZIP
- A firm, level spot, with blocks or gravel under the corners

A small, simple shed you build yourself can come out ahead. A bigger space, or a shed that needs a slab and a hired crew, can tip the other way. The only way to know is to add up your own list.

## Why late fall makes building harder

Building a shed in the fall is very doable. But a few things get harder as the weather turns cold. These are general facts. Your local building department sets the rules for your address.

### Concrete does not like the cold

Some sheds sit on a concrete slab or concrete footings. Concrete needs the right conditions to harden well.

The American Concrete Institute (ACI) treats it as cold weather for concrete when the air drops to **40°F** or is expected to while the concrete cures. In cold weather, builders take extra steps to protect fresh concrete, like covering it and keeping it warm while it cures. Fresh concrete can be damaged if it freezes before it gets strong. And concrete should not be poured on frozen ground.

None of that makes a fall slab impossible. It just adds steps, time, and sometimes cost.

### Footings go below the frost line

When the ground freezes, water in the soil is pulled toward the cold and freezes into layers of ice. Those layers grow and push the soil up. This is called **frost heave**. It can lift and crack things that sit in the soil.

That is why building codes often say footings must go below the frost line. That is the depth the ground is expected to freeze in your area. In our part of the country, that depth is measured in feet, not inches, and it is not the same in every town. Your local building department can tell you the number.

Here is the catch for fall. Digging is easy in soft ground. Once the top of the ground freezes, digging gets a lot harder.

Many small sheds do not need deep footings at all. The model residential code most states build on exempts small detached buildings like most backyard sheds, and many sit on a gravel pad, blocks, or wood skids. Check the plans for your shed and the rules for your address.

### The days get short

Daylight saving time ended on **Sunday, November 1, 2026**. Now that clocks have fallen back, it gets dark earlier in the evening. If you build on weeknights after work, you lose a lot of light. Cold, wet, and dark is not a fun mix for a weekend project, either.

## A container shows up already built

This is the big difference in the fall. A shipping container is not a project you build. It arrives as one finished steel box.

It also does not need a slab. A container rests on its four corners. A common setup is a concrete block, a railroad tie, or a paver under each corner. A gravel pad works too. There is no digging below the frost line and no concrete to cure. Want it set on blocks? Say so in your quote request, so it can be planned before the truck shows up.

The spot does need to be **firm and level**. A level box lets the doors open and latch the right way. Keeping it up off the ground also helps the underside dry out between rains. Our [delivery page](/delivery/) explains why level matters so much.

One more tip for our region. Winter ground can freeze, thaw, and freeze again. That can shift the ground a little under any corner. In the spring, open and close the doors. If they start to stick, check the low corner and re-level it.

## Where a shed wins

Let's be fair. A shed is the better choice for a lot of people. Here is where it shines:

- **Looks.** A shed can match your house. Paint, trim, and a roof shape that fits the yard. A container looks like what it is: a steel box.
- **Windows and a normal door.** Sheds often come with windows and a door you can walk through like a room. We do not add windows, doors, or other changes to containers.
- **Tight spots.** A shed can be built by hand in a small backyard behind a fence. A container needs a long, clear path for the delivery truck.
- **Neighborhood rules.** Local codes and HOAs can treat sheds and containers differently. Check yours either way.
- **Custom sizes.** You can build a shed to fit an odd corner.

Also, many shed sellers deliver ready-built sheds. Those skip the build, just like a container does.

## Where a container wins

A container is built for a hard life on ships and docks. Here is where it shines in a backyard:

- **Steel all around.** Steel walls, steel floor frame, and steel doors you can lock with a strong padlock.
- **Weather and pests.** We stock **used Wind and Water Tight (WWT)** containers. WWT means the box keeps out rain, wind, snow, and pests. The doors seal and there are no holes. Expect some surface rust, dents, or scuffs. That is normal. Our [condition guide](/condition/) shows what to expect.
- **No build.** No kit, no crew, no concrete, no waiting for dry weekends.
- **Room.** A [20ft](/shipping-containers-for-sale/20-foot-shipping-container/) or a [40ft](/shipping-containers-for-sale/40-foot-shipping-container/) holds a lot of stuff. Our [size guide](/size/) helps you pick.
- **It can move.** A container is not fixed to the ground, so it can be moved by truck later if your plans change.

## Winter storage tips for either one

A shed and a container both get cold inside in winter. Neither one is heated. A few habits help:

- **Keep things off the floor.** Use shelves, pallets, or sealed plastic totes.
- **Watch for moisture.** A closed steel box can get water drops on the ceiling when damp air meets cold steel. It looks like a leak, but most of the time it is condensation. Here is [why a container "rains" inside](/blog/why-does-my-container-rain-inside-condensation/) and how to fix it.
- **Never use a fuel heater inside.** Wind and water tight is not airtight, and fumes can build up. Read more in [are shipping containers airtight?](/blog/are-shipping-containers-airtight/)
- **Leave some things out.** Some items do not handle cold well. See [12 things to never store in a shipping container](/blog/12-things-never-store-in-a-shipping-container/).

## Rules: check before you buy or build

A shed or a container may need a permit. It depends on where you live. Things like size, how close it sits to the property line, and how long it stays can all matter. Your HOA may have its own rules too.

**We do not decide whether you need a permit.** Rules are set by your city, your county, and your HOA. Checking them is the buyer's job. Please do it before you buy or build.
